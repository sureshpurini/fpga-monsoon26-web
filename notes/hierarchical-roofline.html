Hierarchical Roofline Analysis

A 3×3 filter, walked up the memory pyramid

L2 supplement · FPGA — Designing High-Performance Kernels · Monsoon 2026

In lecture we drew one roofline: a single slanted HBM roof (316 GB/s) and a flat compute roof, with vadd sitting far down the memory slope because it touches each byte once and has nothing to reuse. This note picks a kernel that does have reuse — a 3×3 image filter — and shows that the honest picture is not one roofline but a stack of them, one per memory tier. The same kernel has a different arithmetic intensity against HBM, against BRAM, and against registers, and optimization is the act of moving reused data up the pyramid until the slow roof (HBM) stops being the one that binds you.

The one idea: a kernel does not have an arithmetic intensity — it has one per level of the memory hierarchy. Draw a roof for each level; the achievable performance is the lowest roof your data placement leaves you under. Caching a reused operand on-chip raises its level's intensity and lifts you off the HBM roof.

The kernel, and why it is the right example

A 3×3 filter computes each output pixel from a 3×3 window of the input:

// O = 3x3 filtered I ;  W = 9 filter weights
for (int i = 0; i < H; i++)
  for (int j = 0; j < W_; j++) {
    int acc = 0;
    for (int u = 0; u < 3; u++)
      for (int v = 0; v < 3; v++)
        acc += W[u][v] * I[i+u][j+v];   // 9 multiply-accumulates
    O[i][j] = acc;
  }

Work per output pixel = 9 MACs = 18 operations (we count a MAC as 2 ops — one multiply, one add — to match the roofline convention from lecture).

What makes it the right teaching example is that its three operands have wildly different reuse, so each one wants to live in a different tier:

Operand Amount of data Reuse (times each value is used) Wants to live in
Filter weights 9 values — tiny every output pixel (millions of times) registers
Image pixels the whole image 9× (each pixel falls in 9 windows) BRAM
Output pixels the whole output 1× (written once, never re-read) streams to HBM

Matching each operand to a tier by its (size, reuse) is the entire optimization — and it is exactly what the three stages below do, one operand at a time.

Assumptions (so the numbers stay clean). 8-bit pixels, weights, and output — 1 byte each, which is realistic for images and plays to the FPGA's love of low precision. A large image, so we ignore borders and startup. 1 MAC = 2 ops.

The three roofs, in three numbers (U50)

From the lecture's memory pyramid — the same UltraScale+ Alveo U50 — here are the three bandwidth roofs we will care about:

Tier Bandwidth (aggregate, on-chip parallel) Note
HBM (off-chip) 316 GB/s but a single m_axi port sees only ~10 GB/s — full BW needs banking across 32 channels
BRAM (on-chip) ≈ 3.6 TB/s hundreds of independent blocks, each with its own ports
Registers ≈ 65 TB/s fully spatial — effectively free bandwidth for tiny, hot data
compute roof ≈ 3.6 TOP/s (INT) a DSP does 1 MAC/cycle; INT8 packing doubles it to ~7.1 TOP/s

Bandwidth rises ~200× from HBM to registers while capacity falls ~40,000×. That inversion is why the game is "load a reused value up the pyramid once, then hit it from the fast roof many times."

Stage 1 — everything from HBM, every time

The naive kernel reads all nine weights and all nine window pixels straight from HBM for every output pixel, and writes the result back:

  • HBM bytes per output = 9 image reads + 9 weight reads + 1 write = 19 B
  • ops per output = 18

AI (vs HBM) = 18 ops ÷ 19 B ≈ 0.95 op/B

That is vadd territory: deep in the memory-bound region. Its ceiling is the HBM roof — about 0.95 × 316 GB/s ≈ 0.30 TOP/s at full bandwidth (and only ~0.95 × 10 GB/s ≈ 9.5 GOP/s if the ports collapse to one HBM channel). The DSPs are almost idle; the kernel is starving for bytes it keeps re-fetching.

Stage 2 — put the weights in registers

The nine weights are constant across the whole image, so load them once into registers and read them from there forever after. They vanish from the HBM byte count (nine one-time reads, amortized to ~0 over a large image):

  • HBM bytes per output = 9 image reads + 1 write = 10 B

AI (vs HBM) = 18 ÷ 10 = 1.8 op/B

The intensity roughly doubled — the weights were half the HBM reads — for the price of nine registers. Ceiling ≈ 1.8 × 316 GB/s ≈ 0.57 TOP/s. We are still HBM-bound, but now the bottleneck is unmistakably the image traffic: every pixel is still being read nine times from HBM, once for each window it belongs to. That redundancy is Stage 3's target.

Why registers, not BRAM, for the weights? They are tiny (9 values) and the hottest data in the kernel — read every cycle. Registers give ~65 TB/s and single-cycle access; spending nine of them to erase a whole operand from HBM is the best trade in the kernel.

Stage 3 — hold the image in BRAM

Now bring the image on-chip: as the window slides, keep the few rows it needs in BRAM (a line buffer — see the box below). Each pixel is loaded from HBM exactly once, parked in BRAM, and then read nine times from BRAM as the window passes over it. Two tiers now carry traffic, and the kernel has a different intensity against each:

Against HBM — per output, only 1 new pixel comes in and 1 result goes out:

AI (vs HBM) = 18 ÷ 2 = 9 op/B

Against BRAM — the nine reuse-reads now land on BRAM:

AI (vs BRAM) = 18 ÷ 9 = 2 op/B

Read those two lines carefully — they are the whole point of a hierarchical roofline:

  • The HBM intensity jumped 1.8 → 9 (a 5× lift), because the image is now loaded once instead of nine times. Ceiling ≈ 9 × 316 GB/s ≈ 2.8 TOP/s — up against the compute roof, ~10× faster than Stage 1.
  • The BRAM intensity is only 2 op/B — lower than the HBM number! — but it is measured against a ~3.6 TB/s roof, so its ceiling is ≈ 2 × 3.6 TB/s ≈ 7.2 TOP/s, comfortably above the compute roof.

So after Stage 3 the BRAM roof has headroom to spare, and the thing now holding the kernel back is the HBM stream-in/stream-out (2.8 TOP/s) bumping against the compute roof (3.6 TOP/s). The memory hierarchy did its job: it moved the binding constraint off the cheap, redundant HBM refetches and up near the compute limit.

The hierarchical roofline, in one picture

Read it like this:

  • Each memory tier is its own slanted roof (perf = AI × bandwidth); higher bandwidth ⇒ higher line. The flat compute roof caps them all.
  • The three orange dots are the three stages, plotted at their AI_HBM. They march up the HBM roof: 0.95 → 1.8 → 9, climbing from ~0.3 TOP/s toward the compute roof as we cache more reuse on-chip.
  • Stage 3 also has a point on the BRAM roof (AI_BRAM = 2), sitting above the compute roof — i.e. once the image lives in BRAM, its bandwidth is not the limit. The attainable performance is always the lowest roof you are under: min(compute, AI_HBM×BW_HBM, AI_BRAM×BW_BRAM).

The move that matters is not "make AI bigger" in the abstract — it is change which roof binds you. Stage by stage we lift off the HBM roof; by Stage 3 the HBM roof has been pushed up next to the compute roof and BRAM is nowhere near binding.

Match each operand to a tier

The whole optimization is this diagram: route each operand to the tier its reuse earns. Weights (reused everywhere) go to registers; image pixels (reused 9×) go to BRAM; the image and output (touched once) just stream through HBM. Every arrow you can delete from HBM — by holding a reused value one level up — raises AI_HBM and slides you off the slow roof.

The scoreboard

Stage What we cached HBM B/out AI vs HBM AI vs BRAM Binds on Ceiling (aggregate)
1 nothing 19 0.95 — HBM ~0.30 TOP/s
2 weights → registers 10 1.8 — HBM ~0.57 TOP/s
3 + image rows → BRAM 2 9.0 2.0 HBM ↔ compute ~2.8 TOP/s

Same silicon, same arithmetic — ~10× more attainable performance, bought entirely by where the reused data lives.

How Stage 3 actually keeps the image in BRAM — the line buffer (teaser). You do not store the whole image on-chip. A 3×3 window only ever needs three rows at a time, so you keep a line buffer — three BRAM row-buffers and a 3×3 shift-register window — and slide it across. Each incoming pixel is written to BRAM once and shifts through the window over the next three rows, feeding the nine MACs from on-chip. That sliding-window / line-buffer datapath is L4/L5 (on-chip reuse, streaming, #pragma HLS ARRAY_PARTITION for the window); here we only needed its effect on the byte counts.

Takeaways

  1. A kernel has one arithmetic intensity per memory tier, not one overall. The hierarchical roofline draws a roof per tier and you live under the lowest one your data placement leaves you against.
  2. Optimization = moving reused data up the pyramid so the slow (HBM) roof stops binding. You are not chasing FLOPs; you are chasing which roof binds.
  3. Route operands by (size × reuse): tiny-and-hot → registers; medium-reuse → BRAM; touch-once → stream through HBM. The 3×3 filter needs all three.
  4. The last wall is honest: Stage 3 still streams every pixel through one HBM path (~10 GB/s per port). Banking across HBM channels to reclaim the full 316 GB/s — and the line buffer that realizes the BRAM reuse — is L4.
